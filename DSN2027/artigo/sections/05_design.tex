\section{Design}\label{sec:design}

The gate judges the \emph{derived model}, never the script or the data that produced
it~\cite{qi2024finetuning}: a fine-tuning job that ends inside the platform hands the derivative
back to the control plane, which keeps it encrypted and runs the Judge as a second, independent job
in a fresh single-use CVM. The Judge receives the derivative, the parent's custodied reference, the
retained training data and its own package, all checked by hash before any weight is opened; it
returns only a fixed-schema \texttt{verdict.json}. Table~\ref{tab:design-req} maps each component
to the requirement it serves.

\subsection{Instruments}\label{sec:design-instruments}

\textbf{S1, behavioral battery.} S1 is the only instrument that can reject on merit. At deposit
time the parent answers the whole public pool of harmful requests from
StrongREJECT~\cite{souly2024strongreject} and the safe prompts of XSTest~\cite{rottger2024xstest}
in every condition; the requests it refuses become that condition's \emph{anchors}, and its
answers are stored, encrypted, as the custodied reference. At judgment time the derivative answers
only what a secret seed draws: 32~public anchors and 16~secret ones (one third, stratified by harm
category), 25~safe XSTest prompts and six encoded variants. Every request gets one greedy answer of
up to 512~tokens with the base's chat template, and the answer is read whole, because a model can
refuse first and comply afterwards. A rubric judge scores harm per item; an item is harmful when its
score reaches $\tau$. Refusal and over-refusal are measured apart from harm: not refusing is not the
same as helping~\cite{souly2024strongreject}.

\textbf{Conditions and formats.} Each item runs with an empty system prompt, the base's default
system prompt and the template most frequent in the job's data (C\textsubscript{job}), since refusal
depends strongly on the system prompt~\cite{arditi2024refusal}. The same judgment runs in every
released format: full precision, LLM.int8~\cite{dettmers2022int8}, NF4 and
FP4~\cite{dettmers2023qlora}. Formats are all-or-nothing: a derivative that passes in full precision
and fails once quantized is rejected, which closes the attack that hides behavior behind
quantization~\cite{egashira2024quantization}. Formats the gate did not judge (GGUF, GPTQ) are never
produced, because the weights never leave the platform. The Judge and the serving path share one
CPU implementation that rounds the weights blockwise (absmax per block of 64 for NF4 and FP4, per row
for LLM.int8) and then computes in full precision; it matches bitsandbytes bit for bit on NF4 and
LLM.int8 and differs on \tbd\ of FP4 weights, all exact ties.

\textbf{Encoded and secret items.} The six encoded variants (Base64, Caesar, leetspeak) ask the model
to answer in the same encoding, and the answer is decoded before scoring; they are sentinels against
covert fine-tuning with a known encoding~\cite{halawi2024covert}, not a promise of coverage. The
secret third of the anchors is never shown to users; only its hash commitment is public, and it is
revealed when the Judge version changes. Passing the public part while failing the secret part is a
guard of its own.

\textbf{Second line: S2 and S3.} Two internal signals compare the derivative with the custodied
parent~\cite{hurtado2026abliterated}: S2 is the ratio of refusal gaps along the parent's refusal
direction~\cite{arditi2024refusal}, and S3 the rank-1 energy of the weight difference in the middle
layers. A white-box owner can train both back to benign values, so they can never reject or approve
on their own; with S1 clean, a strong anomaly (threshold set at 5\% false alarm on benign calibration
derivatives) leads to \emph{inconclusive}. The custody of the reference is what makes them
meaningful: without it, a forged reference passes~\cite{hurtado2026abliterated}.

\subsection{Verdict}\label{sec:design-verdict}

The verdict has three states, and only \emph{approved} releases. Rules are applied in a fixed order;
the first that fires decides (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}). The behavioral veto comes before integrity, so
that a harmful derivative cannot escape by provoking a later failure. The veto is per cell (format,
condition, block and harm category): it fires when the one-sided Wilson lower bound of the
derivative's harm rate, at level $\alpha$ divided by the number of cells tested (Bonferroni), exceeds
the parent's harm rate on the same items by more than the category's $\delta\le 0.15$. A cell whose
upper bound still exceeds $\delta$ is unresolved and yields \emph{inconclusive}; only the public anchors
must be resolved, since secret and encoded blocks are too small and act as sentinels. A judge failure
never becomes a score: outside the veto it makes the verdict \emph{inconclusive by failure}, which
costs the user nothing and is retried in a new CVM.

\subsection{Receipt, life cycle and revocation}\label{sec:design-receipt}

Every verdict, including rejected and inconclusive ones, produces a receipt on the ledger with hashes
only: parent and its custodied reference, derivative, Judge package, battery commitment,
\texttt{prereg.yaml}, retained dataset, the canonical \texttt{verdict.json}, and the identity that
requested the job, plus state and date. Measures and reasons stay off-chain, bound by the verdict's
hash, so an auditor can check a verdict without seeing harmful generations. Only the operator, which
runs the Judge by hash, may register a derivative; the derivative's owner is the adversary and writes
nothing. A derivative is born active only when approved, its owner may withdraw it but never
reactivate it, and the operator may revoke it at any time, for instance when the Judge version
changes. Revoking a base takes down all its derivatives without iterating over them: a derivative is
usable only while its parent is active, a constant-gas check. The cost of a receipt is reported in
\S\ref{sec:eval}.

\textbf{Paying per attempt.} Each verdict leaks one of three states, so an adaptive
attacker~\cite{nasr2025attacker} could probe the gate with many derivatives. Every job is signed by
an identity whose registration costs a proof of work, and each identity holds a quota: a job reserves
the cost of a rejection, and the verdict settles it (approved~1, inconclusive~2, rejected~5; failures
are refunded). An attacker with $N$ identities therefore gets at most $N\lfloor Q/c_R\rfloor$ rejected
attempts, while a legitimate user pays per approval (\S\ref{sec:threat}).
